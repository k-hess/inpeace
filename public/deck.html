The companies that came before us.

A few companies have tried this space. Their stories split cleanly into who failed and who survived, and the survivors have something in common.

Cake absorbed

A consumer end-of-life planning platform: checklists, wishes, guidance. Never charged consumers for the after-death side. Acquired by a funeral-services conglomerate in 2024.

Everplans left consumer

Farewill distressed sale

NOKBOX surviving

view story →

Empathy partner-led

After-loss software that works, sold 99% through life insurers and employers rather than to families. $162M raised, most of the top-10 life insurers as customers. The moment is valuable; reaching it directly is the hard part.

view story →

No consumer-direct attempt has survived standalone: absorbed, pivoted to B2B, or sold distressed. And notably, none of them ever charged consumers for the executor stage as software. The ones doing that today, at $99 to $499 billed to the estate, are small and alive. The path we're describing hasn't failed. It's barely been tried, and the survivors all point the same way: win on a name people trust and on distribution, not category search.

What we learned from BabyList.

The registry is the acquisition funnel, not the business. The revenue is first-party retail, advertising, their fastest-growing line, and insurance-billed product like Babylist Health and breast pumps, $750M in revenue in 2025. Given that model, here's what transfers to us and what doesn't.

What transfers

  • Collaborative features that let people help in different ways. A registry of needs, meals, rides, money. Someone claims a way to help, it's held for them, and if they go quiet it reopens on its own. The family never has to chase anyone.
  • Payment facilitation with no fees builds trust. BabyList built the whole way to give money toward a fund and takes nothing from it. We do the same: contributions toward the funeral, a meal fund, a memorial gift, facilitated right in the app the way any payment processor would, with no fee line for us. Trust is the return.

What doesn't

  • The joy, inspiration, and energy. BabyList gets nine months of happy browsing. A death gives us 24 to 72 hours, once, under duress. There is no energy to browse and nothing to upsell into.
  • The retail-driven experience. Pushing product at a sensitive time feels wrong, and it wouldn't work anyway. Retail, advertising, and insurance-billed product are BabyList's $750M, and none of them exist in grief. The honest comparison is Ever Loved, at $3M.

Affiliate revenue on non-time-critical goods is viable later. Time-critical fulfillment, a casket for a scheduled funeral, is out permanently.

The starting path.

Three stages of demand, and we treat them differently on purpose. Free where attention is cheap and plentiful. Paid where the moment is dense and nobody else is competing for the searcher.

One data structure serves all three doors: the same inventory, gathered ahead of time or acted on after. Same object, different verb.

This is the arbitrage: acquire attention where it's cheap and plentiful, with free experiences and our own reach, and earn revenue where the money is dense and nobody is competing for the searcher.

Where the money comes from.

The free layer builds trust and usage. The paid layer is the part free tools can't do. We benchmarked free AI on real executor scenarios: it handles generic advice, and fails on state deadlines, jurisdiction, warnings before irreversible mistakes, and remembering your case. That line is the business.

Always free

The crisis-week guidance, the "it can wait" pacing, funeral costs and rights, grief support routing, and a basic plan. Charging for advocacy would poison it. This is the trust layer, and it feeds everything below.

What people pay for

Guided help through an estate: a human tier at a service price. Executor expenses are reimbursable from the estate, so the estate pays, not the family. The executor toolkit: computed state deadlines, the case tracker, the shared family view, and later filled court documents and attorney handoff. Whether this is sold self-serve or bundled into guided is what the interviews decide. Advisor referral: a fee-only introduction at the inheritance moment, recurring per funded referral.

Why the executor, when pre-need has the 1.3M in search volume? Because that volume is demand for attorneys, insurers, and documents, and the companies that chased it with software are the earlier slide. The executor is different in kind: a job that can't be deferred, legal deadlines that create urgency, and estate money to pay with. This is the arbitrage: acquire attention where it's cheap and plentiful, with free experiences and our own reach, and earn revenue where the money is dense and nobody is competing for the searcher. The pre-need audience is the pipeline; the executor is the customer.

What we're deliberately parking.

Marketplace / funeral-home directory

Referral fees at the funeral stage are small, state anti-steering laws apply, and the trust cost is high.

Sponsored placement

Paid placement next to a funeral decision would end trust in the brand.

Credential storage

Recording where access lives captures most of the value with none of the breach risk.

Bank linking

Consent law has no path for a deceased person's accounts. Revisit in planning mode.

Our own community

Established free grief communities already exist. Partner with Comfort Club instead of competing.

AI chat concierge

Free chatbots score poorly on executor scenarios, and the errors are the costly kind.

Subscriptions

A one-time-use product has near-zero retention. The ripple loop is the better answer.

Parked, not lost. Each one has a written reason, and each can be reopened when the reason stops applying.
